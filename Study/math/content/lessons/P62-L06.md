## 每一步都重新标出 100%

本课价格均为假想，忽略税费、运费与额外优惠，不需要询价或购买。折扣、涨价与变化率都要先找参照原量，再区分“变化了多少”与“变化后多少”。中文“打八折”表示**支付原价的 80%**，相当于降价 20%，不是减去原价的 80%。

若原价是 x，降 p% 后剩 **x×(1−p/100)**；涨 p% 后为 **x×(1+p/100)**。关系来自原价的一整份加上或减去变化份额，不能把百分数前面的数字直接当钱。

### 例 1 · 节省额与新价格不同

1. 假想原价 **NZ$80**，降价 **20%**。先标 **100%=NZ$80**。
2. 节省额 **80×20/100=NZ$16**。
3. 新价 **80−16=NZ$64**，保留原价的 **80%**。
4. 可以直接算 **80×0.8=64**，但要说明 0.8 是保留比例，0.2 是减少比例。
5. 若问“节省多少”，答十六元；若问“新价多少”，答六十四元。两者单位都是钱，但问题对象不同。

把六十四元涨回八十元，需要增加十六元。相对六十四元的涨幅为 **16÷64=25%**，不是 20%。反向变化的百分率不能简单照抄，因为参照价格改变了。

### 例 2 · 同率降涨不会抵消

1. 第一步仍是 **NZ$80 降 20%→NZ$64**。
2. 第二步按新价涨 20%，重新标 **100%=NZ$64**。
3. 增加的钱是 **64×20%=NZ$12.80**，不是原来减少的十六元。
4. 最终 **64+12.80=NZ$76.80**。也可算 **80×0.8×1.2=76.8**。
5. 相对最初八十元，最后保留 **96%**，总共下降 **4%**。相同百分率作用于不同整体，金额就不同。

工作台原价以整数分保存，选择的价格和 5% 步长使两次变化都能精确到分，不会悄悄四舍五入。先核对第一步的变化额与新价，再按按钮把新价作为下一步参照。改原价、方向或百分率会清空旧步骤。0% 不改变价格；100% 降价会降到零，从零出发无法用有限相对涨幅恢复正价格，不能用除以零算恢复率。

**变化率用哪个分母？** 价格从 NZ$50 涨到 NZ$65，增加十五元，相对原价的涨幅为 **15÷50=30%**。65÷50=130% 描述新价相当于原价多少，不是“涨了 130%”；15÷65 又改用了新价作分母，回答另一个问题。

**百分比与百分点：** 一个假想比例从 20% 到 30%，直接相差 **10 个百分点**。若问“相对于原比例增长百分之几”，则算 **(30%−20%)÷20%=50%**。十个百分点与 50% 相对增长可同时成立，参照问题不同。这里仅比较给定比例，不从中推断真实群体变化或调查结果。

**家庭挑战：** 在纸上给同一假想 NZ$80 标签设计降 20% 与降 25% 两张优惠卡，分别写节省与新价。再给第一张的新价涨 20%，标出两次的 100%，解释为何不回原价。无需购物、询价或付款。

**家长陪学：** 解释必须指出每一步参照、变化额与最终量；百分点还需分清直接差和相对变化的分母。允许画双数轴、列乘法或用钱数说明，不用关键词自动评分。**带走一个发现：** 百分率必须依附参照整体；连续变化时，先更新整体再计算。

## Label 100% again at every step

All prices here are fictional, with no tax, delivery or extra offers. No price inquiry or purchase is needed. Discounts and increases require a starting reference, and “how much changes?” differs from “what is the final price?” The Chinese expression for paying eighty percent of the original price means **20% off**, rather than an 80% reduction.

If the original price is x, reducing it by p% leaves **x×(1−p/100)**. Increasing it by p% gives **x×(1+p/100)**. These relationships add or remove a proportional part from one full original amount; the coefficient alone is not a sum of money.

### Example 1 · Savings differ from the final price

1. A fictional **NZ$80** price falls by **20%**. Mark **100%=NZ$80**.
2. Savings are **80×20/100=NZ$16**.
3. The final price is **80−16=NZ$64**, retaining **80%** of the original.
4. Directly calculating **80×0.8=64** is valid: 0.8 is the retained proportion, while 0.2 is the removed proportion.
5. Answer sixteen dollars for savings, but sixty-four dollars for the final price. Both use money units but refer to different quantities.

Restoring sixty-four dollars to eighty requires a sixteen-dollar increase. Relative to sixty-four, that increase is **16÷64=25%**, rather than 20%. A reverse change does not automatically reuse the same rate because its base changes.

### Example 2 · Equal discount and increase rates do not cancel

1. First, **NZ$80 reduced by 20% becomes NZ$64**.
2. Next increase the new price by 20%, relabeling **100%=NZ$64**.
3. The added amount is **64×20%=NZ$12.80**, rather than the sixteen dollars removed earlier.
4. The result is **64+12.80=NZ$76.80**, or **80×0.8×1.2=76.8**.
5. This retains **96%** of the original eighty dollars, a net decrease of **4%**. Equal rates on different bases change different amounts.

The workshop stores prices in integer cents. Its selected prices and 5% rate steps keep both changes exact to the cent, without silent rounding. Reveal the first change and final price, then use that new price as the next base. Changing the original price, direction or rate clears the stages. A zero rate changes nothing. A 100% discount reduces the price to zero; no finite relative percentage increase from zero can restore a positive price, and division by zero cannot define a recovery rate.

Which denominator defines change? From NZ$50 to NZ$65, the increase is fifteen dollars, so the rate relative to the original is **15÷50=30%**. The quotient 65÷50=130% describes the new price as a percentage of the old, not an increase of 130%. Dividing fifteen by sixty-five instead uses a different reference.

Percentage points ask another question. A fictional rate changing from 20% to 30% rises by **ten percentage points** directly. Its relative increase from the original rate is **(30%−20%)÷20%=50%**. Both statements are valid because they describe different comparisons. These are given proportions, not evidence for a real population or survey conclusion.

**Family challenge:** draw 20% and 25% discount cards for a fictional NZ$80 label. Write each saving and final price, then raise the first discounted price by 20%. Identify both 100% bases and explain why the original price is not restored. No purchase, price inquiry or payment is required.

**For a parent:** look for each step’s base, change and final amount. For percentage points, distinguish the direct difference from relative change and its denominator. Accept diagrams, multiplication or explicit money reasoning rather than keyword matching. **Takeaway:** a percentage needs a reference whole; update that whole before a successive change.
