## 因数把它分完，倍数把它重复

本课先研究正整数的正因数与正倍数。一个正整数能不留余数地整除另一个，就构成因数关系。4×6=24 说明四与六都是二十四的因数，也说明二十四是四与六的倍数；读关系时要把方向说清。

完整相等行帮助看到整除：总计数是行数乘每行数量。这里每格只计一个对象，不代表平方厘米或实际测量面积。能换一种阵列摆法，原来的总数量仍要保留，不能为了凑一幅好看的图丢掉余下对象。

### 例 1 · 二十四的完整因数对

1. 从 **1×24** 开始，再找 **2×12、3×8、4×6**。
2. 每对都能乘回二十四，且两个数都能整除二十四。
3. 列出两端，排序得到 **1、2、3、4、6、8、12、24**。
4. 这是八个不同正因数；不能只写每对较小的一端。
5. 继续到另一方向会重复原有配对，不必把 6×4 与 4×6 当新增因数。

### 例 2 · 平方数的中间因数不重计

1. 三十六的因数对是 **1×36、2×18、3×12、4×9、6×6**。
2. 前四对每对给两个不同因数。
3. 最后一对两端都是六，只贡献同一个因数一次。
4. 不同正因数为 **1、2、3、4、6、9、12、18、36**，共九个。
5. “五对所以十个”把六重计了；数不同因数与数配对不是同一个目标。

### 正倍数列表可以继续

七的正倍数从七开始：7、14、21、28、35……第 k 个正倍数是 7×k，因此第五个是三十五。0=7×0 也是七的零倍，但零不是正数，不能把它当第一个正倍数使序号错位。

正整数的正因数不超过它自己，数量有限；正倍数可以一直增加，没有最后一个。工作台只展示前八个正倍数并用省略号说明还能继续，不能把短列表当完整集合。后续最小公倍数课会按最小正公倍数的约定排除起点零。

二十五张每袋六张，装满四袋后还剩一张：**25=6×4+1**。六不是二十五的因数，因为不能整除。普通除法的精确商也不是直接写成四，四只是满袋数，余量必须说明。

一的正因数只有一。零的情况不能照搬有限正因数表，本课不给零列这种表，也不以除零测试因数。讨论时先说明数的范围，避免把适用于正整数的列表规则扩展到所有整数。

工作台按给定总数生成每一个计数格，选择因数对或交换行列时格数保持。请先自己列配对，再展开完整因数列表核对。若行数不是因数，就不能声称是一幅没有余量的完整阵列。

**带走一个发现：** 我能用乘回与余数零说明因数关系，区分有限因数表与可以继续的倍数表，并说清所问的正数范围。

## Factors divide exactly; multiples repeat a quantity

This lesson studies positive factors and positive multiples of positive integers. A positive integer dividing another with no remainder is its factor. From 4×6=24, four and six are factors of twenty-four, while twenty-four is a multiple of both. State the direction clearly.

Complete equal rows represent total count as rows times quantity per row. Each tile counts one object rather than square centimetres or measured area. Rearrangement retains every object, including leftovers rather than discard them to make a neat picture.

### Example 1 · Complete factor pairs for twenty-four

1. Begin with **1×24**, then **2×12, 3×8 and 4×6**.
2. Every pair multiplies back to twenty-four and divides it exactly.
3. Include both ends and sort: **1,2,3,4,6,8,12,24**.
4. These are eight distinct positive factors, not only the smaller member of each pair.
5. Reversing 4×6 to 6×4 repeats existing factors rather than adds new ones.

### Example 2 · Do not duplicate a square’s middle factor

1. Thirty-six has pairs **1×36, 2×18, 3×12, 4×9 and 6×6**.
2. Each of the first four pairs gives two distinct factors.
3. The last pair has equal ends, contributing six once.
4. Distinct factors are **1,2,3,4,6,9,12,18,36**, nine altogether.
5. “Five pairs, therefore ten factors” duplicates six. Counting pairs differs from counting distinct factors.

### Positive multiples can continue

Seven’s positive multiples begin 7,14,21,28,35… . Positive multiple number k is 7×k, so the fifth is thirty-five. Zero equals 7×0 and is the zero multiple, but not a positive number or the first positive multiple.

Positive factors do not exceed their positive integer and form a finite list. Positive multiples keep increasing with no last member. Eight displayed values and an ellipsis are a window rather than a complete set. Least-common-multiple work later uses the least positive value, excluding the initial zero.

Twenty-five cards in full bags of six give **25=6×4+1**. Six is not a factor of twenty-five because a remainder persists. Four is the full-bag count, not the exact ordinary quotient; retain the remainder.

One has only positive factor one. Zero cannot use this finite positive-factor-list treatment, and division by zero cannot test a factor. State the number domain before extending a list rule.

The workshop generates every counter from the total. Choose a pair or swap rows and columns without changing count. List pairs yourself before checking the full factor list. A nonfactor cannot define a complete equal-row arrangement without leftovers.

**Takeaway:** use multiplication back and zero remainders to justify factor relationships, distinguishing finite factors from continuing multiples and naming the positive-number domain.
