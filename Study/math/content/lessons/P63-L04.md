## 每一行都保留两列的同一关系

假想配方 A:B=2:3 表示两种连续水量的比较。做双倍需要 4 L 与 6 L，做三倍需要 6 L 与 9 L。表格不是把两列各自随意增加，而是每一行两项都用**相同倍数**缩放原配方。

两项相差一，并不能唯一描述 2:3 的关系。4:5 也相差一，但第一种量占第二种量的比例已经不同。同加保持差，通常不保持比；同比要看共同倍数、正行比值或交叉乘积。

### 例 1 · 共同倍数与同加的对比

1. 原行 **A=2 L、B=3 L**。
2. 同乘二得到 **4 L、6 L**，两列共同倍数都是二。
3. 交叉检查 **4×3=6×2=12**，保持原比。
4. 若两项同加二，得到 **4 L、5 L**，交叉乘积 **4×3=12** 与 **5×2=10** 不等。
5. 因此 4:6 是同一配方，4:5 不是。数值都变大、差都好看，仍不能代替同比证据。

把表中 A 从二升增加到八升，乘数为四，所以 B 必须是三升的四倍，即十二升。也可先求每一升 A 需要 3/2 L 的 B，再乘八；这里比较顺序与列名不能丢。

### 例 2 · 共同倍数也可以小于一

1. 原配方仍为 **A:B=2:3**，现在 A 只需 **1/2 L**。
2. A 的共同倍数为 **(1/2)÷2=1/4**。
3. B 同乘四分之一，得 **3×1/4=3/4 L**。
4. 核查 **(1/2)×3=(3/4)×2=3/2**，关系保持。
5. “比值表只能填整数倍”并不成立；连续量允许分数倍，前提是两列用同一个倍数。

例如 A:B=4:6 时，A 为十升，对应共同倍数 2.5，B 为十五升。先把原比化成 2:3 也能算，但不必强制先化简：共同倍数作用在原行上同样有效。

**零行单独解释：** A=0、B=0 表示没有配制，满足 B=(3/2)A 的零量关系；但 0:0 没有可定义的除法比值。不要用“0÷0=3/2”证明等值比，也不要因为不能相除就说零量不符合给定的乘法关系。一个为零而另一个为正，则不符合这个正配方。

工作台列出四行共同缩放的记录，再让你调整候选行。默认 4、5 是一个需要检查的反例；把 B 改为六，观察交叉乘积。修改配方或候选量会清空旧反馈。零、零的候选行有自己的说明，不与正常等值比行混为一谈。

**单位与条件：** 表中两列均以升为单位，并且假定原配方必须保持、无额外固定加入量。若另加一升固定配料，关系会改变，不能沿用同一张表。后面的图像课将把“固定加入量”作为正比例的反例。

**家长陪学：** 请孩子为一行指出两列各自的倍数，再尝试用另一种方法核查。认可分数倍、单位比值或交叉乘积，不能只背“表里每行相差几”。**带走一个发现：** 同一比值表保留两列的共同缩放关系，允许正的分数倍；零点需要用乘法关系单独解释。

## Preserve the same relationship in every row

A fictional recipe A:B=2:3 compares continuous quantities of water. Doubling it needs 4 L and 6 L; tripling needs 6 L and 9 L. Each table row applies **one shared multiplier** to both original terms instead of increasing the columns independently.

A difference of one does not uniquely describe the ratio 2:3. The ratio 4:5 has the same difference but a different quotient. Equal addition preserves a difference, usually not a ratio. Use shared scaling, positive-row quotients or cross products as evidence.

### Example 1 · Equal scaling versus equal addition

1. The original row is **A=2 L, B=3 L**.
2. Multiply both by two for **4 L, 6 L**.
3. Check **4×3=6×2=12**, preserving the ratio.
4. Adding two to both instead gives **4 L, 5 L**, with unequal products **4×3=12** and **5×2=10**.
5. Thus 4:6 is the same recipe and 4:5 is not. Larger values or a familiar difference are not sufficient evidence.

If A changes from two to eight litres, its multiplier is four, so B must be four times three: twelve litres. Alternatively, each litre of A needs 3/2 L of B. Multiply that rate by eight, retaining the column order and meaning.

### Example 2 · The shared multiplier can be below one

1. Keep **A:B=2:3**, but require only **1/2 L** of A.
2. A’s multiplier is **(1/2)÷2=1/4**.
3. Apply it to B: **3×1/4=3/4 L**.
4. Check **(1/2)×3=(3/4)×2=3/2**.
5. Ratio tables are not limited to integer multiples. Continuous quantities permit fractional multipliers, provided both columns share the same one.

For A:B=4:6 and A=10 L, the multiplier is 2.5, so B=15 L. Simplifying first to 2:3 also works, but is not compulsory: shared scaling works directly on the original row.

A zero row needs separate interpretation. A=0 and B=0 means no mixture and satisfies the multiplication relationship B=(3/2)A. However, 0:0 has no defined division quotient. Do not claim that 0÷0 proves a ratio of 3/2. Conversely, inability to divide does not invalidate the zero quantities in the given multiplication rule. One zero quantity with the other positive would fail this recipe.

The workshop lists four scaled rows and then lets you adjust a candidate. The initial 4,5 row is a counterexample to examine. Change B to six and compare cross products. Changing the recipe or candidate clears feedback. The 0,0 candidate has its own explanation rather than being labeled an ordinary equivalent ratio.

Both columns use litres, and the stated recipe has no fixed extra amount. Adding one fixed litre would change the relationship and require a different table. The graph lesson later uses a fixed addition as a counterexample to direct proportion.

**For a parent:** ask for each column’s multiplier, then request a second check. Fractional multipliers, unit quotients and cross products are welcome; a memorized row difference is not enough. **Takeaway:** an equivalent ratio table preserves shared scaling, including positive fractional multipliers. Explain the zero point through multiplication separately.
