Frequently Asked Questions: Rule 605 of Regulation NMS (April 1, 2026)

April 1, 2026

These staff FAQs will be effective on August 1, 2026 (the compliance date for the amendments to Rules 600 and 605 of Regulation NMS, published on April 15, 2024, at 89 FR 26428).

The staff of the Division of Trading and Markets (the “Staff”) of the Securities and Exchange Commission (“Commission”) has prepared the following responses to questions concerning Rule 605 of Regulation NMS (“Rule” or “Rule 605”). These responses represent the view of the Staff. They are not a rule, regulation, or statement of the Commission. The Commission has neither approved nor disapproved this content. These responses, like all staff statements, have no legal force or effect: they do not alter or amend applicable law, and they create no new or additional obligations for any person. Additional information on Rule 605 can be found in the 2024 Rule 605 Amendments Adopting Release, available at: https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2024/34-99679.pdf.[1]

1. Format of Reports and Procedures for Making Monthly Reports Publicly Available

a. Format and Procedures

Q1: What format and procedures should market centers, brokers, and dealers follow in preparing their monthly reports and making them available to the public?

A1: The format of detailed monthly reports required under Rule 605(a)(1) and procedures for making them available to the public are spelled out in the National Market System Plan Establishing Procedures Under Rule 605 of Regulation NMS (“Rule 605 NMS Plan” or “Plan”), available at https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nms/2025/34-103939-appendix.pdf. In general, it requires market centers, brokers, and dealers to make their monthly reports available in the form of electronic data files (standard, pipe-delimited ASCII format). The Rule 605 NMS Plan also sets forth in detail the required contents of the electronic data file, including the identity and order of the 55 fields of information that the file must contain. A description of each of the fields may be found in Exhibit A to the Rule 605 NMS Plan.

Files for detailed monthly reports required under Rule 605(a)(1) shall have separate records for each combination of security, order type, and order size by which a market center, broker, or dealer must categorize its report (a maximum of 240 records for each individual security).[2] The end of each record shall be designated by a carriage return line feed. If there are no orders on which a market center, broker, or dealer must report during a month for a specific combination of security, order type, and order size, no record for such combination need be displayed. If there is no data for a particular field within a record, the field shall be left empty.

The electronic data files containing market center, broker, or dealer reports must be made available for downloading without charge from an internet site (“Download Site”). Under Section VIII of the Rule 605 NMS Plan, each market center, broker, or dealer will be responsible for making arrangements with a self-regulatory organization (“SRO”) that is a party to the Plan to act as the market center, broker, or dealer’s “Designated Participant.” Designated Participants are responsible for assigning a unique reporter identification code (which must be set forth in Field 2 of a market center, broker, or dealer’s electronic data file)[3] to each market center, broker, or dealer for which it acts as Designated Participant.

In addition, each market center, broker, or dealer must notify its Designated Participant of the hyperlink to the market center, broker, or dealer’s Download Site. In turn, each Designated Participant will maintain an internet site (“Link Site”) that includes a comprehensive list of hyperlinks to the respective Download Sites for all of its market centers, brokers, or dealers. In this way, anyone who wishes to download all market center, broker, or dealer reports for each month can be assured that, if they visit the Link Sites of all Designated Participants, they will find hyperlinks to files containing all market center, broker, or dealer reports.

The technical specifications for how a market center, broker, or dealer must prepare the monthly summary report required under Rule 605(a)(2), along with the CSV schema and PDF renderer, are available on the Commission’s website at https://www.sec.gov/data-research/structured-data/taxonomies-schemas/technical-specifications-schemas under “Order Execution Summary (OES) Data Report.”

[Modified:  April 1, 2026]

b. Vendor or SRO Assistance in Making Reports Available

Q2: Does Rule 605 require each market center, broker, or dealer to make its monthly reports available to the public in a separate file on its own internet site, or is it permissible for a market center, broker, or dealer to arrange with other entities (such as a vendor or an SRO) for assistance in making the reports available?

A2: A market center, broker, or dealer need not make its detailed or summary reports available on its own internet site. Market centers, brokers, or dealers have the option to make arrangements with other entities to perform the functions necessary to make their reports available to the public. Nor must each report be downloadable in a separate data file. Thus, for example, an SRO may make the monthly detailed reports for each market center, broker, or dealer for which it acts as Designated Participant available in a single file. Section VIII of the Rule 605 NMS Plan establishes procedures that will allow the public to determine the name of the file containing each market center, broker, or dealer’s detailed monthly reports and the location where the file can be downloaded. Section IV of the Commission’s technical specifications for the summary report required by Rule 605(a)(2), as referenced in FAQ 1, sets forth the procedures for making the summary reports available for downloading and publicly accessible.

[Modified:  April 1, 2026]

c. Calendar Month Reporting

Q3: Should the monthly Rule 605 report encompass all trading days that fall within a calendar month, as opposed to settlement days that fall within the calendar month?

A3: The Rule 605 monthly report should encompass all trading days that fall within the calendar month. Thus, orders received and executed on April 30 must be reported in the reporting entity’s April report, even though trades were not settled until May.

[Modified:  April 1, 2026]

2. Reporting Entities

a. Definition of Market Center – Multiple Trading Venues

Q4: Firm X acts both as an exchange specialist and as an OTC market maker. How should Firm X report under Rule 605?

A4: Firm X operates as two separate market centers and must report separately (with a separate reporter identification code) on trading in each venue.

The definitions in paragraph (b) of Rule 600 of Regulation NMS (“Rule 600”) set out the different types of market centers that must report under Rule 605. The term “market center” is defined as “any exchange market maker, OTC market maker, alternative trading system, national securities exchange, or national securities association.” An “exchange market maker” is defined as “any member of a national securities exchange that is registered as a specialist or market maker pursuant to the rules of such exchange.” An “OTC market maker” is defined as “any dealer that holds itself out as being willing to buy from and sell to its customers, or others, in the United States, a national market system security for its own account on a regular or continuous basis otherwise than on a national securities exchange in amounts of less than block size.”

Accordingly, Firm X, because it acts both as an exchange specialist and as an OTC market maker, would be considered two separate market centers under Rule 605 and must report separately on its trading in each venue. The same result would hold if a firm acted as a specialist on two different exchanges. Rule 605 is intended, among other things, to provide investors and the public with order execution information that will assist them in making order routing decisions. Different exchanges have different trading rules and systems that can affect order execution quality, as do firms when they act as OTC market makers. It therefore is important that monthly reports under Rule 605 reflect these differences.

[Modified:  April 1, 2026]

b.     Integrated Broker-Dealer Firms

Q5: Firm X is an integrated broker-dealer that acts as an OTC market maker in 500 NMS stocks and also accepts orders in other NMS stocks solely as agent for routing to other market centers for execution. In addition, Firm X sometimes receives orders in its 500 market maker stocks that, either because of specific customer instructions or the nature of the particular customer (for example, an advisory client), Firm X is required to route as agent to another market center for execution. How should Firm X report its activities under Rule 605?

A5: As an integrated broker-dealer, Firm X would need to distinguish between Firm X’s activity as an OTC market center and Firm X’s activity as a broker-dealer. For orders in NMS stocks for which Firm X does not act as an OTC market maker, Firm X would not be acting as a market center in those NMS stocks and therefore need not include in its monthly report that it prepares as a market center the orders in those NMS stocks that it receives as agent and routes elsewhere for execution.

The orders that Firm X receives from any person in the 500 stocks in which it acts as an OTC market maker (and therefore is a market center) generally must be included in Firm X’s monthly reports, even if Firm X ultimately routes some of the orders to other market centers for execution. These orders would be reflected in the “shares executed at any other venue” statistic required by Rule 605(a)(1)(i)(F) and otherwise would be reported in exactly the same way as if the order were executed at Firm X.

If, however, Firm X receives orders in NMS stocks for which it acts as an OTC market maker that Firm X is required to route as agent to another market center for execution (either because of specific customer instructions or the nature of the particular customer—it is, for example, an advisory client), then Firm X would not act as a market center for these types of orders and need not include them in the monthly reports that Firm X prepare as market center.

In addition, if Firm X is a broker-dealer that introduces or carries 100,000 or more customer accounts through which transactions are effected for the purchase or sale of NMS stocks, then Firm X also would be required to prepare a separate report for its broker-dealer function. In its broker-dealer Rule 605 report, Firm X would need to report on all orders that it receives for execution as a broker-dealer, whether or not the orders are in NMS stocks for which Firm X acts as an OTC market maker.

[Modified:  April 1, 2026]

c. OTC Market Makers—Fractional Share Issues

i. Fractional Share Component of Notional Value Order

Q6: Is a customer-facing broker-dealer that principally facilitates the trading of fractional share orders solely in the context of handling the fractional share component of notional value orders required to publish a separate Rule 605 report as a market center? What if this broker-dealer is not posting quotes for the customer to execute against and does not seek to profit from the bid-ask spread in executing the fractional share portion of a notional value order?

A6: A customer-facing broker-dealer that principally facilitates the trading of fractional share orders in the context of handling the fractional share component of notional value orders would be considered an OTC market maker (and therefore a market center) and would produce a separate Rule 605 report to reflect such activity.

A firm may act as an OTC market maker for certain types of orders but not others. A notional value order may be split into whole share and fractional share components and a firm may act as an OTC market maker for one or both of these component parts. In some instances, a broker-dealer that receives notional value orders from its customers may choose to execute principally the fractional share component of the order and to route as an agent the whole share component of the order for execution elsewhere. To the extent that the customer-facing broker-dealer executes principally the fractional share components of notional value orders, the firm would be acting as an OTC market maker with respect to the fractional share orders. Whether or not the customer-facing broker-dealer falls within the definition of OTC market maker would not depend on whether it posts quotes for execution or profits from the bid-ask spread.

[New:  April 1, 2026]

ii. Fractional Share Position from Dividend Reinvestment Program or Stock Dividend

Q7: Is a customer-facing broker-dealer required to publish a separate Rule 605 report as a market center if it executes fractional share orders only where a customer has a fractional share position resulting from (1) the customer’s participation in a dividend reinvestment program or (2) a stock dividend with a fractional component?

A7: No. The Commission has exempted from the requirement to produce a separate Rule 605 report as a market center any customer-facing broker-dealer that executes customers’ fractional share orders only in circumstances in which the customer has a fractional share position resulting from (1) the customer’s participation in a dividend reinvestment program or (2) a stock dividend with a fractional component.[4] In such cases, a customer-facing broker-dealer may facilitate its customer’s ability to exit out of such fractional share positions by executing the customer’s order to sell these fractional shares. The customer-facing broker-dealer would not be required to produce a separate Rule 605 report as a market center on the basis of this activity.

[New: April 1, 2026]

d. Exemption for Small Market Centers

Q8: Are any small market centers exempt from the Rule?

A8: The Commission has exempted from the Rule certain small market centers that do not focus their business on the most actively traded securities.[5] Small market centers often may be significant sources of liquidity in the securities of small and medium companies, but have fewer transactions than large market centers over which to distribute the cost of generating the monthly execution quality reports. The Commission therefore is concerned that the costs of compliance for small market centers, as well as the potential costs if small market centers cease supplying liquidity, potentially could outweigh the benefits of their monthly reports. If a small market center chooses, however, to focus a significant part of its business in very actively traded securities, it should compete on the same terms as larger market centers that trade those securities.

The exemption covers any market center that reported fewer than 200 transactions per trading day on average over the preceding six month period in securities that are covered by the Rule (that is, national market system securities that do not qualify for the inactively traded security exemption), but only if more than 90% of such transactions were in securities that are not included in the Nasdaq-100 Index or the S&P 500 Composite Stock Price Index. Once a market center’s average daily reported transactions for the preceding six-month period reach 200, or the percentage of its reported transactions in Nasdaq-100 and S&P 500 securities reaches 10% or greater for the preceding six-month period, the market center would cease to qualify for the exemption. The market center then would be required to begin collecting the requisite order data for the following full month and make its monthly report for that month publicly available by the end of the next month thereafter. For example, if the market center ceased to qualify for the exemption based on trading during the six-month period from January through June, the market center must begin collecting order data for August and make its monthly report (covering August) publicly available by the end of September.

A broker-dealer that routes orders to a market center that does not generate monthly execution quality reports continues to have a duty to monitor the quality of executions received to obtain the best execution of those orders.

[Originally Issued:  June 22, 2001]

3. Covered Orders and Scope of Reportable Orders

a. Definition of Covered Order

i. Order Submitted With a Stop Price

Q9: The first part of the definition of “covered order” in Rule 600(b) provides that covered order means any market order or any limit order (including immediate-or-cancel orders) received by a market center, broker, or dealer during regular trading hours at a time when a national best bid and national best offer is being disseminated and after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security, and, if executed, is executed during regular trading hours. The second part of the definition of “covered order” relates to certain non-marketable limit orders and explicitly states that this includes “an order submitted with a stop price.” Does the first part of the definition of “covered order” also encompass an order submitted with a stop price?

A9: Yes. The reference in the first part of the definition of “covered order” to any market order or limit order includes any market order or limit order submitted with a stop price.

[New: April 1, 2026]

ii. Order That is Not Fully or Partially Executed

Q10: Can an order be a covered order even if it is not fully or partially executed?

A10: Yes. Under the definition of “covered order” in Rule 600(b), an order that remains unexecuted, in whole or in part, may still be a covered order. See FAQ 30 for discussion of the treatment of orders left unexecuted and uncancelled at the end of regular trading hours.

[New: April 1, 2026]

b. Special Handling Exclusions

i. General

Q11: The definition of “covered order” in Rule 600(b) specifically excludes a number of types of orders for which the customer requests special handling and therefore are not covered by Rule 605. Are there other types of orders that fall within the special handling exclusion, such as fill-or-kill and average price orders?

A11: The definition of covered order in Rule 600(b) does not specifically identify every type of order that may fall within the “special handling” exclusion. Among other reasons that an order may qualify for the special handling exclusion, in general, any market or limit order for which the customer requests a type of handling that may preclude the order from being executed promptly at the current market price at the time of order receipt (subject only to a limit price) would qualify for the special handling exclusion and not be covered by Rule 605. Accordingly, fill-or-kill orders are excluded because they must be executed only at their full size (“executed only at their full size” is one of the specific examples in Rule 600(b) of a special handling order). In addition, average price orders (understood as orders that are not intended to be executed promptly at the current market price at the time of order receipt but at an average price over a period of time) also would qualify as special handling orders and be excluded from Rule 605.

[Modified:  April 1, 2026]

ii. Directed Orders

Q12: When a broker-dealer receives a directed order from a customer, should that order be included in that broker-dealer’s Rule 605 report? If the broker-dealer routes the directed order to an exchange, ATS, market maker, or other execution venue as directed by the customer, should that order be included in the Rule 605 report of the execution venue that receives the order?

A12: As a general matter, whether an order qualifies for the special handling exclusion from the definition of covered order should be considered from the perspective of the market center, broker, or dealer that is preparing the Rule 605 report and the parameters of the order that it receives for execution.

If a broker-dealer receives a directed order from a customer, the broker-dealer should consider the order as an order that qualifies for the special handling exclusion and not include the order in its Rule 605 report. The broker-dealer must follow the instructions that are part of the directed order and those instructions may cause a delay in executing the order.

In contrast, if the broker-dealer routes a directed order to an exchange, ATS, market maker, or other execution venue at a customer’s direction, the receiving entity should not consider the order as qualifying for the special handling exclusion on the basis that the original order that the routing broker received from its customer was a directed order. Whether or not the receiving entity is aware that this original order was a directed order, the instructions that are part of the directed order do not need to be carried out by the receiving entity. The broker-dealer should include the order in its Rule 605 report unless another reason for exclusion applies.

[New: April 1, 2026]

iii. Short Sale Orders

Q13: In the 2024 Rule 605 Amendments Adopting Release, the Commission stated that it was adopting the position that non-exempt short sale orders will not be considered special handling orders unless a price test restriction under Rule 201 of Regulation SHO is in effect for the security.[6] How should a market center, broker, or dealer report on an order if a short sale price test restriction is not in effect at the time of order receipt, but goes into effect prior to full execution or cancellation of the order?

A13: If a short sale price test restriction is not in effect at the time of order receipt, but goes into effect prior to full execution or cancellation of the order, the market center, broker, or dealer generally should consider the non-exempt short sale order a special handling order and exclude it from the scope of Rule 605 reporting. However, if the order partially executes or is partially cancelled prior to the time that the price test restriction goes into effect, only the portion of the order that persists at the time that the price test restriction goes into effect should qualify for the special handling exclusion and fall outside the scope of Rule 605 reporting.

[New: April 1, 2026]

iv. Pegged Orders

Q14: How should a market center, broker, or dealer report on a non-IOC pegged order? Does this answer change if the order is an IOC pegged order? If the order has a limit price?

A14: A pegged order is an order that has its price automatically set with reference to a specific market benchmark, which is often the NBBO. Generally, a non-IOC pegged order should be considered a special handling order because the order’s price must be continually adjusted after the time of order receipt whenever that benchmark (such as the NBBO) changes and these continual price adjustments may skew execution quality statistics. Whether or not the non-IOC pegged order has a limit price does not change this analysis and, in either case, the order should not be reflected in the market center, broker, or dealer’s Rule 605 report.

An IOC pegged order will never have its price adjusted after arrival because if the order does not execute immediately then it will be cancelled. Therefore, an IOC pegged order should not be considered a special handling order based on the presence of the peg instruction. Instead, the IOC pegged order should be classified with reference to the NBBO at the time of order receipt, as described in examples 14-3 and 14-4 below. Whether or not the non-IOC pegged order has a limit price does not change this analysis.

The following examples describe several scenarios involving a midpoint peg order that is pegged to the midpoint of the NBBO, such that its price may be continually adjusted as the NBBO changes:

Example 14-1: A broker receives a midpoint peg order without a limit price and with a time-in-force of DAY. The order would qualify for the special handling exclusion and fall outside the scope of the firm’s Rule 605 report.

Example 14-2: A broker receives a midpoint peg order with a limit price and with a time-in-force of DAY. The order would qualify for the special handling exclusion and fall outside the scope of the firm’s Rule 605 report.

Example 14-3: A broker receives a midpoint peg order without a limit price and with a time-in-force of IOC. The order would not qualify for the special handling exclusion based on these factors. For instance, a broker receives an IOC midpoint peg order to sell Security A. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $20.51 bid and $20.55 offer. The order should be categorized by order type according to its price when received, which is the midpoint of the NBBO ($20.53). Therefore, the order would be classified as a midpoint-or-better limit order that is immediate-or-cancel.

Example 14-4: A broker receives a midpoint peg order with a limit price and with a time-in-force of IOC. The order would not qualify for the special handling exclusion based on these factors. However, the presence of a limit price may change how the order is classified. Functionally, this order type has two limit prices, the NBBO midpoint and a fixed limit price. Generally, the order should be categorized by order type based on the less aggressive price (i.e., the higher price for a sell order or the lower price for a buy order) because that price acts as the order’s functional limit price. Consider the following scenarios, where the NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $20.51 bid and $20.55 offer:

  • A broker receives an IOC midpoint peg order to sell Security A with a limit price of $20.52. The order should be categorized by order type according to the less aggressive price when received, which is the midpoint of the NBBO ($20.53). Therefore, the order would be classified as a midpoint-or-better limit order that is immediate-or-cancel.
  • A broker receives an IOC midpoint peg order to sell Security A with a limit price of $20.55. The order should be categorized by order type according to the less aggressive price when received, which is the limit price ($20.55). Therefore, the order would be classified as an executable non-marketable limit order that is immediate-or-cancel.
  • A broker receives an IOC midpoint peg order to sell Security A with a limit price of $20.56. The order should be categorized by order type according to the less aggressive price when received, which is the limit price ($20.56). Therefore, the order would be classified as a non-marketable limit order. However, because the limit price of the order is greater than the national best offer, the order would not be executable. Not having executed, the order would immediately cancel back to the customer. Therefore, the order would fall outside the scope of the broker’s Rule 605 report because it was a non-marketable order that never became executable.

v. Other Orders

Q15: How should a market center, broker, or dealer report on a discretionary order or a slide order? How should a market center, broker, or dealer report on orders that contain additional parameters (e.g., minimum quantity or an add liquidity only parameter) that restrict the order from executing immediately?

A15: In general, any parameter placed on an order that may preclude the order from being executed promptly at the current market price at the time of order receipt (with the exception of a limit price) or, in the case of non-marketable order types, at the time the order becomes executable, would cause the order to qualify for the special handling exclusion and thus the order would not be covered by Rule 605 reporting.

If a slide or discretionary order sets forth conditions that would adjust the price at which order may execute, the price at which the order may execute may automatically adjust and thus inclusion of the order in Rule 605 reporting could skew execution quality statistics. Therefore the order would qualify for the special handling exclusion.

vi. Rule 605 and the National Market System Plan to Address Extraordinary Market Volatility (“Limit Up/Limit Down Plan”)

Q16: Should orders be excluded from the statistical reports under Rule 605 when they are received at a time when either the national best bid or the national best offer is outside the relevant price bands published pursuant to the Limit Up/Limit Down Plan?

A16: Yes, for market orders or marketable limit orders. Several of the statistics for market orders and marketable limit orders under Rule 605 are made with reference to the national best bid and national best offer (or the midpoint between them) at the time of order receipt. A national best bid or national best offer at the time of order receipt that lies outside of the price bands published pursuant to the Limit Up/Limit Down Plan does not have an opportunity to be executed and may not provide a useful benchmark for calculating statistics for market orders and marketable limit orders.

Accordingly, market orders and marketable limit orders received during a time when either the national best bid or national best offer is outside of the Limit Up/Limit Down price bands should be classified as requiring special handling and therefore excluded from the definition of “covered order” set forth in Rule 600(b). If, however, the market center, broker, or dealer cancels such an order back to the submitter and the order is subsequently resubmitted at a time when the national best bid and national best offer are within the Limit Up/Limit Down price bands, the resubmitted order would no longer require special handling and would be a covered order, subject to another applicable exclusion under Rule 605.

Non-marketable limit orders will fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting only if they become “executable” as defined in Rule 600(b). In addition, the NBBO at the time that a non-marketable limit order is received is less relevant because the statistics for non-marketable limit orders under Rule 605 generally refer to the national best bid and national best offer at the time that the order first becomes executable, rather than the time of order receipt. Accordingly, non-marketable limit orders received during a time when either the national best bid or national best offer is outside of the Limit Up/Limit Down price bands should not be classified as requiring special handling based on this factor.

See FAQ 21 for discussion of how to determine whether a non-marketable limit order has become executable when either the national best bid or national best offer is outside of the Limit Up/Limit Down price bands.

vii. Rule 611 and Rule 605 – Special Handling of Certain ISOs

Q17: How should market centers, brokers, or dealers treat intermarket sweep orders (“ISOs”) in their Rule 605 reports?

A17: A market center, broker, or dealer should exclude an ISO from its Rule 605 report if the ISO has a limit price that is inferior to the NBBO at time of order receipt (i.e., the limit price is less than the national best bid for sell orders or higher than the national best offer for buy orders). All other ISOs should be included in the market center, broker, or dealer’s Rule 605 report, absent another applicable exclusion from the definition of covered order.

ISOs can be used to achieve a variety of trading objectives, including both the sweep of multiple price levels and a best-price routing strategy. By marking an order as an ISO, the router indicates to the destination trading center that it has simultaneously routed additional ISOs, as necessary, to any better-priced protected quotations. When the limit price of an ISO is equal to or better than the NBBO at time of order receipt, there can be no better-priced quotations elsewhere, and the router is simply seeking an order execution at the best displayed price or better. In contrast, when the limit price of an ISO is inferior to the NBBO at time of order receipt, the customer is effectively instructing the trading center that it can execute the order at a price inferior to the NBBO, even if one or more trading centers are displaying better prices. This instruction constitutes a request for special handling at the trading center that excludes the ISO from the definition of covered order in Rule 600(b). The execution prices of such excluded ISOs are likely to be inferior to the execution prices of orders with the same limit prices that are not ISOs. Consequently, excluding such ISOs from Rule 605 reports should enhance the comparability of order execution quality statistics across different market centers, brokers, or dealers.

viii. Categorizing IOC Orders Transmitted to ATS with a Not Held Instruction

Q18: How should an ATS report on an IOC order that is entered into the ATS with a not held instruction?

A18: The Commission stated in the 2024 Rule 605 Amendments Adopting Release that “[g]enerally, a ‘not held’ order provides the broker-dealer with price and time discretion in handling the order, whereas a broker-dealer must attempt to execute a ‘held’ order immediately.”[7] An immediate-or-cancel instruction that is transmitted with an order indicates that the order should be executed immediately or cancelled. As a general matter, an ATS matches orders entered into the ATS by broker-dealer subscribers in accordance with its established non-discretionary methods. Accordingly, if an ATS subscriber enters its customer order into an ATS with a time-in-force of IOC, the subscriber and its customer should reasonably expect the ATS to match the order immediately, without price or time discretion, and therefore the ATS should categorize the order as held for purposes of Rule 605 reporting.

c. Locked and Crossed Quotes

Q19: In several different contexts, the Rule requires reference to the NBBO disseminated pursuant to an effective national market system plan. How should market centers, brokers, or dealers report on orders whose statistics would be affected by an NBBO that is locked or crossed?

A19: If the NBBO is locked (the best bid to buy equals the best offer to sell), the quotes still should be used for all purposes under the Rule. If the NBBO is crossed (the best bid to buy is higher the best offer to sell), a market center, broker, or dealer should follow the procedures set forth below.

When the NBBO is crossed for a significant period of time, it raises serious questions whether the quotes continue to provide a reliable benchmark for the statistical measures included in the Rule. The Commission therefore has exempted all orders from the Rule that would require reference to an NBBO that has been crossed for 30 seconds or more.[8] In light of this exemption, market centers, brokers, or dealers should follow the following procedure whenever a reference to the NBBO is necessary in connection with market or marketable limit orders, whether at the time of order receipt or the time of order execution:

First, use the NBBO if the quotes are not crossed, or are locked.

Second, if the NBBO is crossed, reject the crossed quotes and use the next-in-time uncrossed NBBO if there has been less than 30 seconds between the last-in-time uncrossed NBBO and the next-in-time uncrossed NBBO.

Third, if there has been 30 seconds or more between the last-in-time uncrossed NBBO and the next-in-time uncrossed NBBO, the affected order is exempted from the Rule and must be excluded entirely from a market center, broker, or dealer’s report (even if the crossed NBBO only affects a partial execution of the order).

In addition, the determination of when a non-marketable limit order (including an order submitted with a stop price) becomes “executable,” as defined in Rule 600(b), relies on a comparison to the NBBO. A non-marketable limit order will fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting only if it becomes executable. To avoid skewing execution quality statistics, the determination of whether a non-marketable limit order has become executable should not occur when the NBBO is crossed. Therefore, if the NBBO becomes crossed before a non-marketable limit order has become executable, a determination that such order has become executable should not be made during any period of time in which the NBBO is crossed. If the NBBO subsequently becomes uncrossed, a determination of whether the order has become executable should resume. If, instead, the NBBO becomes crossed after a non-marketable limit order has become executable, but before the order is executed, then the procedure set forth above should also apply.

The following examples illustrate how the foregoing procedure should apply:

Example 19-1: At 11:08:10.000 a.m.,[9] a market center receives a limit order to buy 100 shares of Security A with a limit price of $20.75. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $20.75 bid and $20.75 offer.

The locked quotes should be used. The order therefore would be classified as a “marketable limit order” as defined in Rule 600(b) because the limit price is equal to the national best offer at the time of order receipt. The midpoint of the NBBO for purposes of calculating an effective spread for the execution is $20.75. If the buy order is executed at a price of $20.75, the execution would be classified as “executed at the quote” as defined in Rule 600(b). If the buy order is executed at any price lower than $20.75, the execution would be classified as “executed with price improvement” as defined in Rule 600(b). The calculation of a realized spread for the execution would be based on the midpoint of the NBBO at the specified time interval[10] after the time of order execution, so that the locked quotes at the time of order receipt would be irrelevant.  However, if the locked quotes persist at the specified time interval, e.g., 50 milliseconds later, the midpoint for purposes of calculating the realized spread would be $20.75.

Example 19-2: At 2:47:20.000 p.m., a market center receives a market order to sell 500 shares of Security A. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $55.40 bid and $55.30 offer. The time of the last uncrossed NBBO was 2:47:05.000 p.m. The next uncrossed NBBO is disseminated at 2:47:30.000 p.m. and is $55.35 bid and $55.35 offer. The market center should reject the crossed quotes at the time of order receipt and instead use the uncrossed quotes at 2:47:30.000 p.m. (even though they were locked) as the NBBO at the time of order receipt. The midpoint of the NBBO for purposes of calculating the effective spread for the order execution is $55.35. If the market order to sell is executed at a price higher than, equal to, or lower than $55.35, the execution will be classified, respectively, as “executed with price improvement,” “executed at the quote,” or “executed outside the quote.”

Example 19-3: Same facts as Example 19-2 except that the next-in-time uncrossed NBBO after the time of order receipt is disseminated at 2:47:40.000 p.m. Because the period between the last-in-time uncrossed NBBO (2:47:05.000 p.m.) and the next-in-time uncrossed NBBO (2:47:40.000 p.m.) is 30 seconds or more, the market order to sell 500 shares of Security A is exempted from the Rule and must be excluded from the market center’s monthly report.

Example 19-4: At 12:59:20.000 p.m., a market center receives a non-marketable limit order to sell 200 shares of Security A with a limit price of $38.60. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $38.50 bid and $38.40 offer. The time of the last uncrossed NBBO was 12:59:05.000 p.m. The next uncrossed NBBO is disseminated at 12:59:30.000 p.m. and is $38.55 bid and $38.55 offer. Between the time of order receipt and the end of regular trading hours, the non-marketable limit order does not at any time become executable and therefore the non-marketable limit order does not fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting, regardless of whether the NBBO is crossed or uncrossed.

Example 19-5: Same facts as Example 19-4, except that at the time an uncrossed NBBO is disseminated at 12:59:30.000 p.m., the NBBO is $38.65 bid and $38.65 offer. Therefore, the non-marketable limit order has become executable. The market center should not make a determination that the order has become executable while the NBBO is crossed and instead use the uncrossed quotes at 12:59:30.000 p.m. (even though they were locked) as the NBBO at the time the order first becomes executable.

d. Trading Halts

Q20: How should orders for a security be handled when trading in the security is halted at the market center either at the time of order receipt (or time a non-marketable limit order becomes executable) or shortly thereafter? Does the answer differ depending on whether the trading halt is a general regulatory halt or occurs solely at the particular market center that received the order?

A20: The manner of reporting on orders under Rule 605 will be the same for all “announced” trading halts. To qualify as an “announced” trading halt, the halt must be either a general regulatory halt or a trading halt that is announced by a market center in accordance with all applicable regulatory rules. Orders that are received prior to and during the time of an announced trading halt should be handled in accordance with the following procedures.

First, all market and marketable limit orders received during the period that trading is halted must be entirely excluded from the Rule 605 monthly report.[11]

Second, a non-marketable limit order received during the period that trading is halted may be included in the Rule 605 report if it becomes executable after trading resumes.[12] In addition, regardless of when the non-marketable limit order is received, a non-marketable limit order should not be considered to have first become executable while trading is halted.

Third, if a market or marketable limit order was received (or a non-marketable limit order became executable) less than five minutes prior to the trading halt and remains outstanding (in whole or in part) at the time of the trading halt, the entire order is exempted from the Rule[13] and should be excluded from the Rule 605 monthly report.

Fourth, for orders that are executed less than five minutes prior to the trading halt, the calculation of average realized spread should use the last NBBO disseminated prior to the time of the trading halt, to the extent that the realized spread time horizon specified in Rule 605(a)(1)(i) would otherwise fall within the time period during which trading was halted.

Fifth, if an order was received five minutes or more prior to the trading halt and remains outstanding (in whole or in part) at the time of the trading halt, the order continues to be covered by the Rule. However, for executions of market orders or marketable limit orders that occur after the end of the trading halt, a market center, broker, or dealer may deduct the time period during which trading was halted from the calculations using the time of execution of the order. For executions of non-marketable limit orders that occur after the end of the trading halt, a market center, broker, or dealer may deduct the time period during which trading was halted from these calculations only if the order became executable prior to the time of the trading halt.

The following examples illustrate how these foregoing procedures should apply:

Example 20-1: At 10:45:30.000 a.m., Market Center X halts trading in all securities because of a serious systems breakdown. It announces the trading halt in accordance with all applicable regulatory rules. At 11:25:30.000 a.m., Market Center X resumes trading in all securities. All market and marketable limit orders received for execution by Market Center X from 10:45:30.000 a.m. until 11:25:30.000 a.m. are exempted from the Rule and must be excluded from Market Center X’s monthly report. However, non-marketable limit orders received for execution by Market Center X during this time would not fall within the scope of this exemption and would be covered by the Rule if they become executable after 11:25:30.000 a.m.

Example 20-2: Same facts as Example 20-1, except that Market Center X receives a marketable limit order at 10:44:45.000 a.m. that is unexecuted at the time trading is halted 45 seconds later. The order is exempted from the Rule and must be excluded from Market Center X’s monthly report.

Example 20-3: Same facts as Example 20-1, except that Market Center X receives a marketable limit order for 500 shares at 10:42:15.000 a.m. that receives a partial execution of 200 shares at 10:42:20.000, with the balance of 300 shares still unexecuted and outstanding at the time of the trading halt. The entire order is exempted from the Rule and must be excluded (including the 200-share execution) from Market Center X’s monthly report.

Example 20-4: Same facts as Example 20-1, except that Market Center X receives an executable non-marketable limit order at 10:44:45.000 a.m. that is unexecuted at the time trading is halted 45 seconds later. The order is exempted from the Rule and must be excluded from Market Center X’s monthly report.

Example 20-5: Same facts as Example 20-1, except that Market Center X receives an executable non-marketable limit order at 10:38:15.000 a.m. that is unexecuted and outstanding at the time of the trading halt. At 11:28:05.000 a.m., after the end of the trading halt, the order is executed.  The order must be included in Market Center X’s monthly report, except that Market Center X may deduct the 40-minute period of the trading halt from the time of execution.

Example 20-6: Same facts as Example 20-1, except that Market Center X receives a non-marketable limit order at 10:38:15.000 a.m. that does not become executable before the time trading is halted 45 seconds later. At 11:28:05.000 a.m., after the end of the trading halt, the order becomes executable. The order executes in full 30 seconds after becoming executable. The order must be included in Market Center X’s monthly report, and no deduction of time should occur as a result of the trading halt because it occurred before the order became executable.

e. Straddle State

Q21: In the 2024 Rule 605 Amendments, the Commission stated that “for purposes of determining when an order first becomes executable, an order generally should not become executable during a time when the underlying security is in a Straddle State.”[14] Does this guidance apply only to an order that is on the opposite side of the quote that triggers the Straddle State?

A21: No. An order should not become executable during a time when the underlying security is in a Straddle State, whether or not that order is on the opposite side of the quote that has triggered the Straddle State.

As discussed in FAQ 16, a national best bid or national best offer that lies outside of the price bands published pursuant to the Limit Up/Limit Down Plan does not have an opportunity to be executed and may not provide a useful benchmark for calculating statistics. Further, the determination of whether an order is “executable” as defined in Rule 600(b) relies on a comparison of an order’s limit price to the national best bid or national best offer on the opposite side of the market. Specifically, a non-marketable buy order will become executable when the limit price is equal to or greater than the national best bid, and a non-marketable sell order will become executable when the limit price is equal to or less than the national best offer.

When a Straddle State is triggered because the national best offer is above the applicable upper price band, a buy order should not be considered executable because the national best offer is intended to represent the best available price at which shares may be bought and this price is unreliable because it is above the upper price band. At the same time, a sell order should not be considered executable because the determination of whether the sell order has become executable would rely on the national best offer, which, as discussed, is not a reliable price.

Conversely, when a Straddle State is triggered because the national best bid is below the applicable lower price band, a buy order should not be considered executable because the determination of whether the buy order has become executable would rely on the national best bid, which would not be a reliable price because it is below the applicable lower price band. At the same time, a sell order should not be considered executable because the national best bid is intended to represent the best available price at which shares may be sold and, as discussed, is not a reliable price.

Example 21-1: At 12:38:40.000 p.m., a market center receives a non-marketable limit order to buy 200 shares of Security A with a limit price of $15.30. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $15.35 bid and $15.45 offer. The current LULD reference price for Security A is $15.30, and the LULD price bands are $14.58 and $16.22. At 12:39:20.000 p.m., the NBBO for Security A is $14.15 bid and $15.55 offer. At this time, the security is in a Straddle State because the national best bid is below the lower price band. The buy order should not be considered executable because the determination of whether the buy order is executable would rely on the national best bid ($14.15), which is not a reliable price because it is below the lower price band.

Example 21-2: At 1:58:32.000 p.m., a market center receives a non-marketable limit order to buy 400 shares of Security B with a limit price of $14.50. The NBBO for Security B at the time of order receipt is $14.55 bid and $14.70 offer. The current LULD reference price for Security B is $14.21 and the LULD price bands are $13.50 and $14.92. At 1:59:56.000 p.m., the NBBO for Security B is $14.65 bid and $14.95 offer. At this time, the security is in a Straddle State because the national best offer is above the upper price band. The buy order should not be considered executable because the national best offer ($14.95) is intended to represent the best available price at which shares may be bought and is not a reliable price because it is above the upper price band.

f. Pre-market and Opening Auction Issues

i. Marketability Prior to Dissemination of Primary Listing Market’s First Firm Uncrossed Quote

Q22: When should the marketability of a limit order received pre-open be determined?

A22: The marketability of a limit order received prior to the opening of regular trading hours should be determined after the opening of regular trading hours from the time that the primary listing market disseminates its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security.

The definition of “marketable limit order” in Rule 600(b) provides, in part, that when an order is received at a time when a national best bid and national best offer is not being disseminated, whether an order is a marketable limit order shall be determined at the time that the national best bid and national best offer is first disseminated during regular trading hours after time of order receipt. This definition further provides that for orders received when the national best and national best offer is being disseminated but the primary listing market has not disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotation in the security (the “interim opening period”), whether an order is marketable limit order shall be determined from the time that the primary listing market disseminates its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security.

The Commission stated in the 2024 Rule 605 Amendments that the changes to the definition of “marketable limit order” to determine whether an order is marketable based on the time that the primary list market disseminates its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security “will move the determination of whether an order is a marketable limit order or a NMLO to a time when the NBBO is more representative of the security’s price than may be the case during the interim opening period.”[15] Accordingly, the determination of whether a limit order is a marketable limit order or a non-marketable limit order should be made from the time that the primary listing market disseminates its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security.

ii. Primary Listing Market Publishes a Quote Before Opening Auction

Q23: In several different contexts, Rule 605 requires reference to the NBBO during regular trading hours and after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security. There are scenarios in which a primary listing market may publish its first quotations prior to conducting an opening auction. When this occurs, should Rule 605 reporting take into consideration the quotations published before the opening auction?

A23: In the 2024 Rule 605 Amendments, the Commission discussed its reasoning for treating orders received or executed during the interim opening period in the same manner as any other order received or executed pre-open or post-close under Rule 605. As part of this discussion, the Commission recognized that “[d]uring this interim opening period, quotations and execution prices may be less representative of the value of the security than during the rest of regular trading hours because . . . quotations disseminated prior to a primary listing market disseminating its first quotations in a security often reflect spreads that vary significantly from the norm during regular trading hours.”[16] The Commission also stated that using a benchmark measured from a time after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security “will help prevent the potential skewing of execution quality statistics caused by reliance on quotations from the interim opening period, and thereby increase the comparability of Rule 605 reports.”[17]

Staff understands that when a primary listing market publishes its first quotations in a security during regular market hours and subsequently conducts an opening auction, those quotations that the primary listing market disseminated prior to the opening auction may be less representative of the security’s price. As such, consistent with the Commission’s stated intention that the first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security that are used for Rule 605 reporting should be representative of the value of the security, reporting entities should rely on a primary listing market’s first firm, uncrossed quotations published after the opening auction (where the primary listing market conducts an opening auction).

g. Stop Orders

i. Conditions for a Stop Order to Become Executable

Q24: If an order is submitted with a stop price and a limit price, how should the determination be made of when the order becomes executable?

A24: The determination of when an order becomes executable when that order has both a stop price and a limit price should take into account both the stop price and the limit price.

The definition of “executable” in Rule 600(b) provides, in part, that executable means, for any order submitted with a stop price, that the stop price has been triggered during regular trading hours and after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security. The definition of “executable” also provides, for non-marketable limit orders other than orders submitted with stop prices, that executability is determined by comparing the limit price to the NBBO.

When an order has a stop price and a limit price, it may be possible for the stop price to be triggered but the order to remain at a price where it would not have an opportunity execute because the limit price is outside of the NBBO. The Commission stated in the 2024 Rule 605 Amendments that “modifying Rule 605’s coverage of NMLOs so that reporting entities are required to disclose execution quality information only for those NMLOs that become executable (i.e., eventually touch the NBBO) will facilitate comparisons between reporting entities, by ensuring that the execution quality statistics for NMLOs more meaningfully capture a market center’s performance in handling NMLOs, rather than reflecting market conditions potentially outside of the market center’s control, such as movements of the NBBO.”[18] Taking into consideration the order’s limit price, in addition to the stop price, when determining whether an order is executable would help investors compare the performance of market centers, brokers, and dealers from a point in time when the order could reasonably be expected to execute.

Therefore, where an order has both a stop price and a limit price, two conditions should be met for the order to become executable. First, that the stop price has been triggered during regular trading hours and after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security. Second, once the stop price has been triggered, that, for a buy order, that the limit price is equal to or greater than the national best bid during regular trading hours and after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security, and for a sell order, that the limit price is equal to or less than the national best offer during regular trading hours and after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security. Further, the time the order becomes executable should be the time that these two conditions are first satisfied.

The following examples illustrate how the foregoing procedure should apply:

Example 24-1: At 12:05:03.000 p.m., a broker receives an order to sell Security A that has a stop condition that will be triggered when the national best bid is $15.00 or lower and a limit price of $16.00. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $15.30 bid and $15.40 offer. At 2:31:17.000 p.m., the NBBO is $15.00 bid and $15.05 offer. At 3:14:32.000 p.m., the NBBO is $15.95 bid and $16.00 offer. The order would have first become executable at 3:14:32.000 p.m. and fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting, and execution quality statistics based on the time that the order first becomes executable should be based on this time and the NBBO at this time.

Example 24-2: Same facts as Example 24-1, except that after the NBBO reaches $15.00 bid and $15.05 offer at 2:31:17.000 p.m., the national best offer remains below $16.00 for the remainder of regular trading hours. The order would have never become executable and therefore fall outside the scope of Rule 605 reporting.

ii. Stop Orders Triggered at Downstream Broker-Dealer

Q25: When a customer-facing broker-dealer receives a stop order from a customer and routes the stop order to a downstream broker-dealer with instructions for the triggering of the stop order, should the stop order be included in the Rule 605 report for each broker-dealer?

A25: Each broker-dealer that receives the stop order for execution should include the order in its Rule 605 report, to the extent the order meets the criteria for Rule 605 reporting. In particular, a stop order becomes “executable” as defined in Rule 600(b) when the stop price is triggered during regular trading hours and after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security. A stop order will not fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting unless it becomes executable, and many of the required execution quality statistics rely on the time that the order first becomes executable or the NBBO at that time.

Staff recognizes that, in many instances, a downstream broker-dealer that receives a stop order may not communicate the time that the stop order is triggered back to the customer-facing broker-dealer. In such instances, the customer-facing broker-dealer (and any other broker-dealer that received the order for execution and then routed the order to another broker-dealer before the stop price was triggered) should utilize available market data to calculate the time at which stop price should have been triggered. The broker-dealer should utilize such time as the time that the order first became executable. The broker-dealer then should utilize market data to determine the NBBO at the time that order first became executable, similar to the treatment of other non-marketable limit orders.

h. Exemption for Inactively Traded Securities

Q26: Is a market center, broker, or dealer required to report on orders in all national market system securities, even those that are inactively traded?

A26: No. The Commission has exempted orders in national market system securities that are inactively traded.[19] The exemption covers any national market system security that did not average more than five reported transactions per trading day, as disseminated pursuant to an effective transaction reporting plan, for each of the preceding six months (or such shorter time that the security has been designated a national market system security). An inactive security will lose its exemption only after its average daily reported transactions have exceeded five for each of the preceding six months. Orders in exempted securities need not be included in the monthly Rule 605 report, but a market center, broker, or dealer is free to include them if it chooses to do so.

4. Executions, Cancellations, and Modifications to Orders

a. Partial Executions and/or Partial Cancellations

i. Generally

Q27: How should a market center, broker, or dealer report on partial executions and partial cancellations of orders under Rule 605?

A27: Rule 605 statistics are designed to handle multiple partial executions and/or cancellations of an order. When a market center, broker, or dealer receives a marketable order, the order is permanently categorized by security/order type/order size immediately upon receipt. When a market center, broker, or dealer receives a non-marketable order, the determination that the order is non-marketable occurs at the time of order receipt, and further categorization by security/order type/order size occurs when the order becomes executable. In either case, all executions and/or cancellations of the order will be included in the fields of information for that single record. Except for the field of information for total number of covered orders (and, for non-marketable order types, the number of covered orders that received either a complete or partial fill), all of the other fields of information required by Rule 605 are reported in terms of number of shares, share-weighted amount, or notional value. Consequently, each partial execution or cancellation can be reflected separately in the statistics.

A non-marketable limit order (including an order submitted with a stop price) will fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting only if it becomes “executable” as defined in Rule 600(b). Accordingly, any cancellation (including a partial cancellation) that occurs prior to the time that the order becomes executable would not be reflected in a market center, broker, or dealer’s Rule 605 report. In the case of a partial cancellation, the categorization of the order by order size would be based on the remaining size of the order at the time that it becomes executable.

Example 27-1: A market center receives a market order to buy 3000 shares of Security A at 3:30:00.000 p.m. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $25.30 bid and $25.60 offer. The market center executes the order in three partial executions: 400 shares at $25.50 at 3:30:08.000 p.m., 1000 shares at $25.55 at 3:30:15.000 p.m., and 1600 shares at $25.60 at 3:31:02.000 p.m.

The market center would report on the 3000 share market order as follows. First, the order, which would have a notional value of $76,800, would be classified at the time of order receipt as falling within the record for Security A/market orders/$50,000 to less than $200,000/at least a round lot. Thereafter, all information relating to the order will be included in one of the fields for that single record. Thus, 400 shares would fall within the field for shares executed from 1 second to less than 10 seconds; 1000 shares would fall within the field for shares executed from 10 seconds to less than 30 seconds; and the remaining 1600 shares would fall within the field for shares executed from 30 seconds to less than 5 minutes.

Next, in calculating effective spread, each partial execution would be treated separately and weighted according to the size of the partial execution. Thus, the effective spread for the first execution would be 10 cents: $25.50 minus $25.45 (the midpoint of the NBBO at the time of order receipt) multiplied by 2; the effective spread for the second execution would be 20 cents: $25.55 minus $25.45 multiplied by 2; and the effective spread for the final execution would be 30 cents: $25.60 minus $25.45 multiplied by 2. The 10 cent effective spread would be weighted by 400 shares, the 20 cent effective spread would be weighted by 1000 shares, and the 30 cent effective spread would be weighted by 1600 shares.

The remaining fields of information required for an order would be calculated in an analogous manner (e.g., a separate realized spread would be calculated for each partial execution).

Example 27-2: Same facts as Example 27-1, except that the final 1600 shares were cancelled by the customer prior to execution. All calculations discussed above under Example 27-1 would remain the same for the first two partial executions, but the final 1600 shares would now simply be included in the field of information for shares cancelled prior to execution.

ii. Partial Execution Pre-Market or During an Auction

Q28: How should a reporting entity report on an order that is partially executed prior to the opening of regular trading hours or during the period between the start of regular trading hours and when the primary listing market disseminates its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security (i.e., the interim opening period)? What about if an order is partially executed during an opening auction or during a re-opening auction?

A28: If an order is partially executed prior to the opening of regular trading hours or during the interim opening period, the portion of the order that remains unexecuted when regular trading hours open and the primary listing market disseminates its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security may be reflected in the reporting entity’s Rule 605 report.

A market or marketable limit order that partially executes prior to regular trading hours or during the interim opening period would not have been received during regular trading hours at a time after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security and thus not be a covered order as defined in Rule 600(b). Thus, the market or marketable limit order would not be reflected in the reporting entity’s Rule 605 report.

A non-marketable limit order will fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting if it becomes executable as defined in Rule 600(b). If a non-marketable limit order partially executes prior to regular trading hours or during the interim opening period, that partial execution would not be included in a reporting entity’s Rule 605 reports. But if the remaining portion of the order subsequently becomes executable during regular trading hours and after the interim opening period, that remaining portion would be subject to Rule 605 reporting.

With respect to an order that partially executes in an opening auction or re-opening auction, the portion of the order that remains unexecuted after the auction would be subject to Rule 605 reporting if it is otherwise a covered order.

Example 28-1: A market center receives an order to buy 500 shares of Security A with a limit price of $10.50 at 8:00:00.005 a.m. At 8:20:00.000 a.m., 200 shares of the order execute. The remaining balance of 300 shares remain unexecuted at the start of regular trading hours at 9:30:00.005 a.m. The order is a non-marketable limit order based on the national best bid when the NBBO is first disseminated during regular trading hours after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security. The order becomes executable at 10:14:30.009 a.m. 300 shares execute at 10:15:00.800 a.m. The market center’s report should reflect the receipt and execution of a 300-share executable non-marketable limit order. Because the 200 shares executed at 8:20:00.000 a.m., outside of regular trading hours, they should not be included in the market center’s Rule 605 report.

b. Modified Orders

Q29: How should a market center, broker, or dealer report under Rule 605 on an order that is modified by the customer after submission, such as when the customer changes the limit price on a limit order, removes the stop price on a stop order, or removes a special handling instruction?

A29: For modifications of any market or marketable limit order, except for a reduction in the size of an order (addressed in FAQ 27 above as a partial cancellation of an order), order modifications should be reported as a cancellation of the original order and a submission of a new order as replacement for the cancelled order. Order modifications to a non-marketable limit order that occur after the order has become executable as defined in Rule 600(b) should be treated in the same manner. In each instance, a market center, broker, or dealer should report on the replacement order (assuming it falls within the definition of covered order) just as it would any other new order.

Order modifications to a non-marketable limit order that occur before the order has become executable would not be reflected in Rule 605 reports because the order would not yet fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting. In such instance, a market center, broker, or dealer should report on the order as modified closest in time prior to when the order becomes executable.

An adjusted or voided order execution is addressed in FAQ 32 below.

c. Orders Left Unexecuted and Uncancelled at End of Regular Trading Hours

Q30: How should a market center, broker, or dealer report on orders received during regular trading hours, but that remain unexecuted and uncancelled at the end of regular trading hours on the day of order receipt?

A30: As a general matter, a market center, broker, or dealer’s report should reflect the disposition of orders during regular trading hours on each individual day that the orders fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting. Orders left unexecuted and uncancelled, in whole or in part, at the end of regular trading hours on the day of order receipt (or, for non-marketable orders, the day the order becomes executable) should be reflected in the market center, broker, or dealer’s monthly report as having been received. For market or marketable limit orders, no further information should be reported concerning the unexecuted part of such orders. For non-marketable limit orders (including orders submitted with a stop price), the market center, broker, or dealer should also calculate statistics for the cumulative number of shares executed regular way, both overall and specifically on a national securities exchange, at prices that could have filled the order while the order was in force, capped at order size (i.e., relative fill rates). In sum, a market center, broker, or dealer’s statistical report should reflect the disposition of orders solely during regular trading hours on the day of order receipt (or, for non-marketable orders, the day the order becomes executable), and each day begins with a clean slate of orders.[20]

Example 30-1: A market center receives a 500 share limit order to buy Security A with a limit price of $30.40 at 10:45:00.000 a.m. on April 30. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $30.40 bid and $30.50 offer, so the order is executable at the time of order receipt (10:45:00.000 a.m.). At 10:45:08.000 a.m., 300 shares of the order are executed at the limit price. The remaining balance of 200 shares is left unexecuted at the end of regular trading hours on April 30.

The market center would reflect the order in its April report as follows. A single executable non-marketable limit order for 500 shares with a notional value of $15,200 would be reflected as having been received. The 300 share partial execution would be included in the field of information for shares executed at the receiving market center and shares executed from 1 second to less than 10 seconds after the time the order becomes executable.  In addition, realized spread statistics, average midpoint, and time-to-execution statistics would be calculated for the partial execution (Rule 605 does not require quoted spread, effective spread, price improvement/disimprovement, and size improvement statistics for non-marketable limit orders). Further, relative fill rate statistics would be calculated based on the full size of the order received.

d. Good-til-Cancelled Orders

Q31: How should a market center, broker, or dealer report on good-until-cancelled (“GTC”) orders that are not fully executed by the end of regular trading hours on the day of order receipt?

A31: For purposes of Rule 605 reporting, a market center, broker, or dealer should treat a GTC order as if it were a new order on each day during which the order persists. The market center, broker, or dealer should reflect the GTC order on its Rule 605 report for each trading day based on what occurs during regular trading hours.

Consistent with the guidance in FAQ 30, GTC orders left unexecuted, in whole or in part, at the end of regular trading hours on the day of order receipt (or, for non-marketable limit orders, the day the order becomes executable) should be reflected in the market center, broker, or dealer’s Rule 605 report as having been received. With respect to the unexecuted portion of the order, no further information should be reported, except for the relative fill rate statistics, if applicable.

On subsequent trading days, a market center, broker, or dealer should determine whether a GTC order with a limit price is marketable and whether the order is a covered order at the start of each trading day during which the order persists. For purposes of determining whether the GTC order is a covered order on a trading day other than the day of order receipt, the order should be considered as having been received before the start of regular trading hours. For orders received outside of regular trading hours, the definition of “covered order” in Rule 600(b) encompasses only those limit orders that are non-marketable limit orders. Accordingly, if a GTC limit order is still in force on a trading day other than the day of order receipt, and would be considered marketable on that day, that order would fall outside the scope of Rule 605 reporting for purposes of that trading day because it would not fall within the definition of covered order.

A non-marketable limit order should be reflected on a market center, broker, or dealer’s Rule 605 report only if it becomes executable as defined in Rule 600(b). For a GTC order that is determined to be a non-marketable limit order for a particular trading day, whether the order is executable should be determined on that day, during regular trading hours and after the primary listing market has disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations in the security. The order should be reflected on the market center, broker, or dealer’s Rule 605 report only as it pertains to those days on which the order becomes executable.

When calculating execution quality statistics that refer to the time an order first becomes executable for a GTC order for a particular trading day, the market center, broker, or dealer should utilize the time that the order first becomes executable on that trading day. There is not a maximum number of days that may elapse between the time of order receipt and the time the order first becomes executable that should cause a GTC order to fall outside the scope of Rule 605 reporting.

The definition of “covered order” in Rule 600(b) provides that to be a covered order, that order, “if executed, is executed during regular trading hours.” A market center, broker, or dealer’s Rule 605 report should reflect the disposition of a GTC order solely during regular trading hours on those days for which the order falls within the scope of Rule 605 reporting. If a GTC executes or partially executes outside of regular trading hours, that execution or partial execution should not be reflected in the market center, broker, or dealer’s Rule 605 report. However, the Rule 605 report should still reflect the disposition of that order during regular trading hours.

Example 31-1: A broker-dealer receives a GTC 500 share limit order to buy Security A with a limit price of $30.50 at 3:58:00.000 p.m. on April 30. The NBBO for Security A at the time of order receipt is $30.40 bid and $30.50 offer. At 3:58:50.000, 300 shares of the order are executed at the limit price. The remaining balance of 200 shares is left unexecuted at the end of regular trading hours on April 30.

The broker-dealer would reflect the order in its April report as follows. A single marketable limit order for 500 shares with a notional value of $15,250 would be reflected as having been received in the order size category $10,000 to less than $20,000 and at least a round lot. The 300-share partial execution would be included in the field of information for shares executed at the receiving broker-dealer and shares executed from 10 milliseconds to less than 1 second after the time of order receipt. In addition, the broker-dealer would include the other statistics required for executions of marketable limit orders.

Example 31-2: The order discussed in Example 31-1 remains open after the end of regular trading hours. 200 shares of the order remain. 100 of these shares execute in after hours trading. Because this execution occurred outside of regular trading hours, it is outside the scope of Rule 605 reporting and the broker-dealer would not report any information with respect to the partial execution.

Example 31-3: The order discussed in Example 31-2 remains open at the start of regular trading hours on May 1. 100 shares of the order remain. The broker-dealer should consider the order as a 100 share order received on May 1, outside of regular trading hours. Consider the following scenarios:

  • The order executes in the opening auction on May 1. Because the order executed prior to the time during regular trading hours that the primary listing market disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations, the order would fall outside the scope of Rule 605 reporting.
  • The order does not execute in the opening auction and the NBBO during regular trading hours after the primary listing market disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations is $30.45 bid and $30.50 offer. The order would be considered a marketable limit order. However, because the order would be considered as having been received outside of regular trading hours, it would not meet the definition of covered order and would not be in scope for Rule 605 reporting for that trading day.
  • The order does not execute in the opening auction and the NBBO during regular trading hours after the primary listing market disseminated its first firm, uncrossed quotations is $30.51 bid and $30.55 offer. At 10:50:15.300 a.m., the NBBO is $30.50 bid and $30.54 offer. The order would then be considered an executable non-marketable limit order and should be reflected in the broker-dealer’s May report as a single executable non-marketable limit order for 100 shares with a notional value of $3,050 in the order size category of $1,000 to less than $5,000 and at least a round lot. If the order subsequently executes or cancels, then the broker-dealer should reflect the required statistics for such execution or cancellation. If the order executes, the time to execution should be measured from the time the order became executable (i.e., 10:50:15.300 a.m.). If the order remains unexecuted at the end of the trading day, no further information should be reported with respect to the order, except for the relative fill rate statistics.

Example 31-4: The order discussed in Example 31-2 remains open on May 1, but does not become executable until September 15. 100 shares of the order remain. On September 15, the order becomes executable at 11:25:10.500 a.m. and executes in full at 11:27:50.125 a.m. The broker-dealer would reflect the order in its September report as follows: a single executable non-marketable limit order for 100 shares with a notional value of $3,050 would be reflected as having been received in the order size category of $1,000 to less than $5,000 and at least a round lot. The 100-share execution would be included in the field of information for shares executed at the receiving broker-dealer and shares executed from 1 second to less than 10 seconds after the time the order becomes executable. The broker-dealer would not have reflected the order in its May, June, July, or August reports, as the order never became executable and thus did not fall within the scope of Rule 605 reporting over the course of those months.

e. Adjusted or Voided Order Executions

Q32: How should a market center report an order execution whose terms are subsequently adjusted or an order execution that is voided entirely?

A32: If the terms of an order execution are adjusted or the execution is voided within a normal settlement cycle, the market center should report on the order in accordance with the terms of execution as they were finally received by the customer.  Orders not adjusted or voided until after the normal settlement cycle should be reported as they were originally executed.

5.   Timestamping

a. Establishing Time of Order Receipt

Q33: How should a market center, broker, or dealer establish the time it receives an order when the order passes through various automated systems before it actually reaches the point at which it is executed? What about an integrated broker-dealer firm that initially receives orders from its brokerage customers?

A33: A market center, broker, or dealer must assign a time of order receipt to its orders at the time they first are captured in an automated order handling system operated by, or on behalf of, the market center, broker, or dealer. For an integrated broker-dealer that receives orders from its brokerage customers, the time of order receipt is the time that it first captures these orders in the firm’s automated order handling system. The time of order receipt is an essential element in calculating many of Rule 605’s statistics, such as assigning quotes as benchmarks for effective spread and price improvement statistics, as well as calculating speed of execution statistics for marketable orders. Rule 600(b) specifies that the “time of order receipt” means the time (at a minimum to the millisecond) that an order was received by a market center for execution, or in the case of a broker or dealer that is not acting as a market center, the time (at a minimum to the millisecond) that an order was received by the broker or dealer for execution. In the Rule 11Ac1-5 Adopting Release, the Commission emphasized that “it is critically important for market centers to assign a time of receipt (including seconds) to orders in a prompt, consistent, and non-manipulatory manner” and that “failure to meet the standard would be a serious violation of the Rule.”[21] Examples of such compliance concerns would include a market center queuing orders without immediately assigning a time stamp or a market center implementing preliminary selection criteria (e.g., to determine whether an order should be retained or routed to another market center for execution) prior to the time that the orders are marked as received and assigned a time of order receipt.

c. Orders Received in the Same Millisecond as a Quote Change

How should a market center, broker, or dealer assign an NBBO to an order that is received during the same millisecond as one or more quote changes?

d. Time of Execution for Stopped or Guaranteed Orders

Q36: Is it permissible for a market center, broker, or dealer to assign an execution time to orders when they are “stopped” or “guaranteed” an execution at no worse than a specific price and later assigned a specific execution price?

A36: Yes. The statistical measures required by Rule 605 may be calculated using the guarantee time as the time of order execution, but only under the following conditions. First, the guarantee must be made pursuant to an SRO rule setting forth the standards for guaranteed or stopped executions. Second, the customer must be immediately informed of the guarantee and the guarantee price. Third, the guarantee must be final with no opportunity for rescission. Finally, the guarantee price must be no worse than the NBBO at the time of order receipt and for the full size of the order.

If guarantee time is used as the time of execution for an order, it must be used for all purposes under Rule 605, including both the calculation of speed of execution statistics and the calculation of average realized spread, as defined in Rule 600(b).

6. Execution Quality Statistics

a. Average Midpoint and Average Spread Statistics

Q37: The Rule 605 statistics for average midpoint, average effective spread, average quoted spread, and average realized spread utilize a share-weighted average. Is this share-weighting based on the number of shares executed? Does each statistic apply only to orders executed in whole or in part?

For purposes of the average midpoint, average effective spread, average quoted spread, and average realized spread statistics, the share-weighting of each order should be based on the number of shares executed. Rule 605(a)(1)(i) and (ii) provide that these statistics apply to executions of covered orders. Therefore, each statistic should apply only to those covered orders that have been executed in whole or in part and consider the number of shares that executed only.

b. Share-weighted Average Percentage Price Improvement

Q38: How should a market center, broker, or dealer calculate share-weighted average percentage price improvement as required under Rule 605(a)(2)(vi) for summary reports?

A38: Rule 605(a)(2)(vi) provides that the share-weighted average percentage price improvement is calculated as the cumulative amount that prices were improved less the cumulative amount that prices were executed outside the quote divided by the sum of the average midpoint times the number of shares executed. Accordingly, a market center, broker, or dealer, should calculate share-weighted average percentage price improvement as follows: (I) – a market center, broker, or dealer should calculate net price improvement, as measured by the cumulative amount that prices were improved less the cumulative amount that prices were outside the quote. (II) – a market center, broker, or dealer should calculate, for each execution, the product of the midpoint and the number of shares executed. (III) – a market center, broker, or dealer should sum the products in (II).[22] (IV) – the market center, broker, or dealer should divide (I) by (III).

Example 38-1: Consider the following simplified example with 4 executions:

  • Trade 1: NBBO at time of order receipt: $20.00-$20.04.
    • Order to buy 300 shares is executed at $20.03.
    • Cumulative amount that the price is improved is 300*$0.01=$3.
    • Midpoint is $20.02.
  • Trade 2: NBBO at time of order receipt: $25.00-$25.04.
    • Order to sell 500 shares is executed at $25.00.
    • Order is executed at the quote.
    • Midpoint is $25.02.
  • Trade 3: NBBO at time of order receipt: $23.00-$23.06.
    • Order to buy 400 shares is executed at $23.08.
    • Cumulative amount that the price is outside the quote is 400*$0.02=$8.
    • Midpoint is $23.03.
  • Trade 4: NBBO at time of order receipt: $21.00-$21.08.
    • Order to sell 350 shares is executed at $21.02.
    • Cumulative amount that the price is improved is 350*$0.02=$7.
    • Midpoint is $21.04.
  • Net price improvement is: Cumulative price improvement (Trade 1($3)+Trade 4($7)) – cumulative amount prices were outside the quote (Trade 3($8))= $2.
  • The sum of the product of the midpoint and the number of shares executed for each execution is: [Trade 1(300*$20.02)+Trade 2(500*$25.02)+Trade 3(400*$23.03)+Trade 4(350*$21.04)]= $35,092.[23]
  • Share-weighted average percentage price improvement is: $2/$35,092=0.005699%.

c. Best Available Displayed Price

Q39: The 2024 Rule 605 Amendments provide that price improvement statistics relative to the best available displayed price will not be required to be reported until six months after the odd-lot order information needed to calculate the best available displayed price is made available pursuant to an effective national market system plan.[24] Prior to this time, may a market center, broker, or dealer produce these statistics using the national best bid (for a buy order) or national best offer (for a sell order) as the best available displayed price?

If an effective transaction reporting plan or effective national market system plan is disseminating best odd-lot order information, a market center, broker, or dealer should use that information to calculate its price improvement statistics relative to the best available displayed price.

d. Size Improvement

Q40: How should reporting entities calculate the total number of shares executed with price improvement and the total number of shares executed at the quote for the size improvement metric required by Rule 605(a)(1)(ii)(S)? If a reporting entity displays all or any part of an order, should order executions that occur after the order is displayed be counted for purposes of the metric?

A40: Under Rule 605(a)(1)(ii)(S), reporting entities must calculate a size improvement metric for marketable order types and midpoint-or-better limit orders. This metric is the sum of, for each execution of a covered order, the greater of: the total number of shares executed with price improvement plus the total number of shares executed at the quote minus the order size benchmark (as defined in Rule 600(b)), or zero (the “size-improved outsized share count”). The definitions of “executed with price improvement” and “executed at the quote” in Rule 600(b) look to the NBBO at the time of order receipt. Therefore, when calculating the size-improved outsized share count, the reporting entity should include the number of shares that executed at a price that was equal to or higher (lower) than the NBB (NBO) at the time of order receipt, in the case of a sell (buy) order.

Whether the order is displayed, in full or in part, before the order is fully executed should not change this analysis. Rather, even if an execution occurs after the order has been fully or partially displayed, the executed shares should be counted as shares executed with price improvement or shares executed at the quote, to the extent that they fall within these terms based on a comparison of the execution price to the NBBO at the time of order receipt.

Example 40-1: At 10:45:10.550 a.m., a market center receives a 1000 share order to buy with a limit price of $10.10. The NBBO is $10.00 by $10.10, and there are 200 shares displayed at the national best offer. The market center executes 700 shares at $10.10 and posts the remaining 300 shares at $10.10. At 10:45:11.005 a.m., the NBBO is $10.10 bid and $10.12 offer. An incoming market order to sell subsequently executes against the resting 300 shares to buy at $10.10. The market center would record an order size benchmark of 200 shares (reflecting the size displayed at the national best offer at the time of order receipt). To calculate the size-improved outsized share count, the market center would count 700 shares as executed at the quote at the time of order receipt. The market center would also count the subsequent execution of 300 shares because this execution occurred at the quote or with price improvement (i.e., the $10.10 execution price was at or lower than the NBO at the time of order receipt ($10.10)). Finally, the market center would report a size-improved outsized share count of 800 shares (1000 shares – 200 shares).

e. Notional Value

Q41: How should notional value be calculated for marketable limit orders?

A41: In the 2024 Rule 605 Amendments, the Commission stated: “Reporting entities generally should calculate a limit order’s notional value by multiplying the number of shares by the order’s limit price. In addition, reporting entities generally should calculate a market order to buy’s notional value by multiplying the number of shares by the national best offer at the time of order receipt and a market order to sell’s notional value by multiplying the number of shares by the national best bid at the time of order receipt.”[26]

However, in the case of marketable limit orders, it may skew the notional value of the order to assign a notional value based on the limit price of the order, especially if the limit price is far through the NBBO. For example, if the NBBO is $10.00 bid and $10.10 offer, a 1000 share market order to buy would have a notional value of $10,100. If the order instead were a 1000 share marketable limit order to buy with a limit price of $13.50, and the limit price was used to calculate the order’s notional value, the order would have a notional value of $13,500. This difference in notional value between the market order and marketable limit order would not be consistent with the expectation that these orders may behave in a similar fashion.

Therefore, it is appropriate to calculate the notional value of market orders and marketable limit orders in the same way, i.e., for buy orders, using the national best offer at the time of order receipt, and for sell orders, using the national best bid at the time of order receipt.

For non-marketable limit orders, the limit price should generally be used to calculate notional value.

f. Relative Fill Rate for Non-marketable Order Types

Q42: The 2024 Rule 605 Amendments provide for the reporting of relative fill rates for non-marketable orders, specifically the cumulative number of shares executed regular way at prices that could have filled the order while the order was in force, capped at the order size. How should a reporting entity account for executions at the same time increment the order is created, executed, or cancelled? Should a reporting entity include its own executions in this count?

A42: For the relative fill rate statistics required by Rule 605(a)(iii)(B) and (C), a reporting entity should include executions that occur during the same time increment the order becomes executable. Likewise, reporting entities should include executions that occur during the same time increment the order is either executed or cancelled (provided the order became executable prior to cancellation). This may include the execution of the order itself.
